# Iman Building: Practical vs Theoretical

## Overview

This comparison examines two approaches to building **īmān** (إيمان — faith) in the believer. Dr. Majdi al-Hilali, in *al-Jīl al-Maw'ūd*, presents a **practical** model: īmān is built through the five foundational knowledges (al-maʿārif al-khamsah), and the depth of knowledge directly determines the quality of dealings with Allah. This is a **knowledge-to-action** pipeline where the governing axiom is "treatment is proportionate to knowledge" (al-muʿāmalah bi-qadr al-maʿrifah).

The **manhaj-oriented** approach, typified in works like *To Be a Muslim* and similar contemporary tarbiyyah curricula, builds īmān through a structured methodology: defining the Muslim's identity, obligations, and mission in a systematic progression from creed to practice to movement. This is a **methodology-to-commitment** pipeline where the framework of understanding determines the scope of engagement.

Both aim at the same destination — a believer whose īmān is living, active, and transformative — but they travel different roads.

## Hilali's Practical Approach

### The Five Knowledges (Al-Maʿārif al-Khamsah)

Al-Hilali's Chapter 3 (*Kayfa Tuḥaqqaq Ṣifāt al-Jīl al-Maw'ūd?*) argues that the ten attributes of the promised generation — ikhlāṣ, maḥabbah, khawf, ʿibādah, tawāḍuʿ, zuhd, jihād, ṣabr, iʿtidāl, ukhuwwah — cannot be assumed or imposed. They are **natural fruits** (thamarāt ṭabīʿiyyah) that grow from five foundational knowledges:

1. **Maʿrifat Allāh** (knowledge of Allah) — the first and most critical axis (p. 102)
2. **Maʿrifat an-Nafs** (knowledge of the self) — knowing one's weakness, ignorance, poverty
3. **Maʿrifat ad-Dunyā** (knowledge of the world) — the dunyā as worthless, transient station
4. **Maʿrifat al-Ākhirah** (knowledge of the Hereafter) — the true destination
5. **Al-Fahm aṣ-Ṣaḥīḥ lid-Dīn** (proper understanding of the religion) — comprehensiveness, balance, priorities, objectives

Each knowledge is not merely cognitive but must be **deep** (ʿamīqah), **firm** (rāsikhah), **continuous** (mustamirrah), **affecting the feelings** (tū'aththir fī al-mashāʿir), and **part of īmān** (juz'an aṣīliyyan min al-īmān) (p. 102). Superficial knowledge produces no change.

### Treatment Proportionate to Knowledge (Al-Muʿāmalah bi-Qadr al-Maʿrifah)

The governing principle is stated on pp. 99–100:

> "If we look at our dealings and relationship with Allah, we find them cold and limited… what governs them is the degree of our knowledge of Him."

Al-Hilali illustrates with two analogies:

1. **Two strangers on a train** (pp. 99–100): Initially formal, they warm as they learn about each other. If one discovers the other holds a prestigious position, his entire manner changes. "This without a doubt reflects on how he deals with him."

2. **Our relationship with Allah** (p. 99): The deficiency in our worship is not a failure of will but a failure of knowledge. "The more the servants' ignorance of Allah increases, the more their incorrect conception of Him grows."

The Quranic foundation: {وَمَا قَدَرُوا اللَّهَ حَقَّ قَدْرِهِ} — "They did not appraise Allah with true appraisal" (al-Anʿām 6:91).

### Knowledge Through Three Means

The five knowledges are acquired through three primary channels (pp. 142–144):

1. **The Qur'an** — the primary instrument, which "introduces the servant to his Lord to the furthest extent that his mental capacity can bear" (p. 146)
2. **Reflecting on life events (aḥāth al-ḥayāh)** — six types of divine messages: mercy, wisdom, warning, support, punishment, and combined (pp. 116–118)
3. **Righteous companionship (ṣuḥbah ṣāliḥah)** — the murabbī (educator) binds and guides all three means

The Prophet ﷺ as the paradigm: "His knowledge of the greatness of his Lord had increased, and this was reflected in his words and his dealing with his Lord" — the story of Mūsā at the mountain (al-Aʿrāf 7:143, p. 101).

### The Heart's Two Forces

Al-Hilali describes the heart as pulled by two forces: **īmān** and **hawā** (desire). At the moment of decision, whichever is stronger prevails. Sin occurs not because īmān is absent, but because hawā temporarily overpowers it — meaning that the knowledge underlying īmān was not deep or continuous enough to sustain the heart at that moment (p. 103). This explains the hadith: "A servant does not commit zinā while he is a believer" (al-Bukhārī) — the act of sin is a momentary eclipse of knowledge.

## The Manhaj-Oriented Approach (To Be a Muslim)

### Identity and Definition

The manhaj-oriented approach, as found in works like *To Be a Muslim* and similar tarbiyyah curricula, begins with **identity definition** — what does it mean to be a Muslim? This is a systematic, top-down approach:

1. **Creed (ʿaqīdah):** The foundational beliefs — tawḥīd, risālah, ākhirah — are defined and enumerated
2. **Obligations (wājibāt):** The specific duties — prayer, fasting, zakat, ḥajj, dawah — are laid out in order
3. **Manhaj (methodology):** The path of collective work — joining the jamāʿah, understanding the stages of dawah, knowing the manhaj of change
4. **Mission (risālah):** The believer's role in the larger project of Islamic revival

The emphasis is on **understanding the framework** of Islam as a complete system — dīn — and the believer's position within it. Īmān is built by understanding what Islam demands, what it prohibits, and what it envisions.

### Systematic Progression

Unlike al-Hilali's organic "knowledge produces attributes" model, the manhaj approach uses a **structured curriculum**:

- **Level 1:** The Muslim's relationship with Allah (creed, worship, character)
- **Level 2:** The Muslim's relationship with the Muslim community (brotherhood, mutual rights, collective obligations)
- **Level 3:** The Muslim's relationship with the dawah (calling to Islam, bearing witness, enjoining good and forbidding evil)
- **Level 4:** The Muslim's relationship with the wider world (jihad of knowledge, economic engagement, civilisational contribution)

Each level builds on the previous one, and the believer progresses through a **defined curriculum** with measurable competencies.

### Commitment Through Understanding

The manhaj approach builds commitment by showing the believer the **bigger picture** — that Islam is not merely a set of personal devotions but a comprehensive system with a civilisational mission. Īmān is strengthened by understanding:

- The historical mission of the Muslim ummah
- The current state of the ummah and its challenges
- The methodology of change and its stages
- The individual's role within the collective project

This produces **directed īmān** — faith that is oriented toward a specific mission and sustained by the clarity of its purpose.

## Key Differences

| Dimension | Hilali's Practical Approach | Manhaj-Oriented Approach |
|---|---|---|
| **Starting point** | Knowledge of Allah (maʿrifah) — the heart's encounter with the Divine | Definition of the Muslim identity — the mind's grasp of the system |
| **Core axiom** | Treatment is proportionate to knowledge (muʿāmalah bi-qadr al-maʿrifah) | Commitment is proportionate to understanding (iltizām bi-qadr al-fahm) |
| **Īmān model** | Knowledge → īmān → attribute → action (organic, from the inside out) | Identity → obligation → manhaj → mission (systematic, from the top down) |
| **Primary concern** | Depth and quality of knowledge — is the heart truly transformed? | Scope and clarity of understanding — does the Muslim know what is required? |
| **Qur'an's role** | The sole formative source that introduces the servant to Allah and links knowledge to life | The primary source alongside the Sunnah, providing the texts and principles for the curriculum |
| **Spirituality** | Heart-centred: īmān as a lived reality producing awe, love, fear, reliance | Mind-centred: īmān as a structured conviction producing commitment, discipline, mission |
| **Practical expression** | Individual transformation visible in dealings: sincerity, love, fear, zuhd | Collective engagement visible in activity: dawah, organisation, institution-building |
| **Companionship** | Righteous companionship as one of three means of acquiring knowledge | Companhip as the jamāʿah — the organised community of believers |
| **Weakness diagnosis** | Ignorance of Allah — "they did not appraise Allah truly" (6:91) | Ignorance of the manhaj — not knowing what Islam requires or how to work for it |
| **Ideal product** | The believer whose heart is so transformed that attributes flow naturally | The Muslim who understands the complete system and commits to its mission |

## Synthesis

The two approaches address **different dimensions** of the same need. Al-Hilali's practical approach addresses the **heart's transformation** — the inner change that makes all external activity meaningful and sustainable. The manhaj-oriented approach addresses the **mind's orientation** — the structural understanding that makes individual effort directed, coherent, and collectively effective.

Without al-Hilali's practical depth, the manhaj approach risks producing believers who understand the framework but whose hearts are cold — who can articulate the mission but whose īmān does not produce the ten attributes. The Prophet ﷺ warned: "Whoever learns knowledge to boast before scholars, argue with fools, or attract people to himself — Allah puts him in Jahannam" (Ibn Mājah, p. 165).

Without the manhaj orientation, al-Hilali's approach risks producing individuals whose hearts are transformed but whose effort is undirected — sincere, fearful, loving believers who lack the structural understanding to channel their īmān into effective collective action.

A synthesis would propose:

1. **Begin with maʿrifah.** The five knowledges must be the foundation — not as abstract curriculum items, but as lived encounters with Allah, the self, the dunyā, the ākhirah, and the dīn. The heart must be transformed first.
2. **Structure through manhaj.** Once the heart is transformed and the attributes begin to emerge, the manhaj provides the framework for directing those attributes toward the collective mission. The transformed heart needs the oriented mind.
3. **Sustain through companionship.** Both approaches recognise the essential role of righteous companionship — whether as the "third means" of acquiring knowledge (al-Hilali) or as the jamāʿah that embodies the manhaj.
4. **Measure by attributes, not activities.** The ultimate test is not how many activities a believer performs, but whether the ten attributes are genuinely present. Al-Hilali's criterion — "treatment is proportionate to knowledge" — is the most reliable measure of whether īmān-building has succeeded.

The promised generation must have both: hearts so transformed that ikhlāṣ, maḥabbah, khawf, zuhd, and ṣabr flow naturally, and minds so structured that those attributes are directed toward the mission of naṣr and tamkīn.
