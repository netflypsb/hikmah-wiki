And after the period of hardship and trials, relief will come — by Allah's permission — and the ummah will begin to unite, gradually. The banners of falsehood (bāṭil) will fall with ease and speed. Here, the generation of change will assume a new role: working to unite the ummah under a single banner, leading it to establish the Islamic caliphate (al-khilāfah al-islāmiyyah), recovering all occupied lands, and rescuing oppressed Muslims in every corner of the earth.

And the role of the promised generation will continue after that in spreading Islam and achieving global leadership (istīdādiyyat al-ʿālam), and realising the promise of the Messenger of Allah ﷺ that this religion will reach the horizons, and that Rome will be conquered, so that the Islamic state thereby becomes the foremost power in the world — spreading security and safety, rescuing humanity from misguidance (ḍalālah), and bringing it out of darkness into light.

Yes, this will happen — by Allah's permission — just as it happened with the first generation, who began as individuals — persecuted, oppressed in Mecca — reading the Qur'an and listening to the verses of the promise of victory and establishment (naṣr wa tamkīn). Their utmost aspiration at that time was to sleep safely, and that no one among them would be exposed to harm. These very people saw establishment with their own eyes, and witnessed the might and glory of Islam. And how wondrous that after this, their fear became for themselves — [the fear of] the trial (fitnah) of the world being spread out before them.

Al-Bukhārī reported in his Ṣaḥīḥ that ʿAbd al-Raḥmān ibn ʿAwf — may Allah be pleased with him — was brought food while he was fasting. He said:

"Muṣʿab ibn ʿUmayr was killed, and he was better than me. He was shrouded in a single garment: if his head was covered, his feet were exposed, and if his feet were covered,

**Drawing Lessons from the Past (al-Istifādah min al-Durūs al-Sābiqah):**

Upon the generation of change (jīl al-taghyīr) is [the duty] not to abandon its mission of leading the ummah, even after it attains sovereignty and global leadership. Rather, it must continue building generations, and guarding them against the diseases of nations — such as luxury (taraf), oppression (ẓulm), and then distance from Allah, Mighty and Majestic, and departure from the circle of His command and His guardianship (wilāyah).

**Summary (Khulāṣat al-Qawl):**

The ummah's need now for the existence of the generation of change — the promised generation of victory and establishment (al-jīl al-mawʿūd bi-al-naṣr wa al-tamkīn) — is a severe and agonising need (ḥājah shadīdah wa māssah) that demands the pooling of all efforts for its formation. And why not, when it is the generation that will breathe life into the ummah

**Translator's notes for Chunk 4:**

1. **ʿAbd al-Raḥmān ibn ʿAwf's weeping — the core paradox:** This is one of the most powerful narrations in the chapter. ʿAbd al-Raḥmān, one of the ten promised Paradise, was brought food while fasting. Instead of eating, he wept — remembering Muṣʿab ibn ʿUmayr (martyred at Uhud, shrouded in a single garment too short to cover his whole body) and Ḥamzah (martyred at Uhud). His fear was not about death or hardship — it was about *prosperity*: that the worldly rewards given to later Muslims might be *in place of* the eternal reward that their martyred brothers received. This is al-Hilali's cautionary tale for the prosperity stage: the greatest danger is not enemy attack but spiritual complacency.

2. **Khabbāb ibn al-Aratt's weeping — the same fear:** Khabbāb, visiting the ailing ʿAbdullāh ibn Masʿūd, was told his brothers (martyrs) would be raised on the Day of Judgment as martyrs. Instead of rejoicing, he wept — saying the martyrs had already received their full wages (ujūrahum), while those who remained feared that their good deeds' reward was being given to them in this world. The phrase "I am not anxious" (amā innahu laysa bi-jazaʿ) means: my weeping is not from weakness or fear of death.

3. **"The diseases of nations" (أمراض الأمم):** Al-Hilali identifies three specific diseases that destroy civilisations after they achieve power:
   - **Taraf (الترف):** Luxury/effeminacy — excessive comfort leading to softness and decay
   - **Ẓulm (الظلم):** Oppression/injustice — turning from justice once in power
   - **Distance from Allah (البعد عن الله):** Spiritual drift — exiting the circle of Allah's authority (wilāyah)
   These three mirror the standard cyclical theory of civilisational rise and fall found in Quranic exegesis and Islamic political philosophy.

4. **"Severe and agonising need" (حاجة شديدة وماصة):** The word māssah (ماصة) conveys an urgent, pressing, almost desperate need — stronger than a regular need. Al-Hilali uses it to stress that the formation of the promised generation is not a luxury or academic exercise, but a survival necessity.

5. **"Rome will be conquered" (فتح روما):** A reference to the Prophet's ﷺ prophecies about the conquest of Constantinople (fulfilled in 1453 by Sultan Mehmed II) and the anticipated conquest of Rome. This is part of the eschatological framework in Islamic tradition about the final triumph of Islam. Al-Hilali references it to argue that the promised generation's mission extends to global leadership.

6. **The chapter's structure — a summary of the summary:** Al-Hilali's closing summary (Khulāṣat al-Qawl) on pages 21-22 recaps the entire argument:
   - The ummah needs the promised generation urgently (the diagnosis)
   - The generation must breathe life and direct sentiment toward real change (the cure)
   - It must lead through hardship and boycott (the ḍarrāʾ stage)
   - It must lead through prosperity, conquests, and global leadership (the sarāʾ stage)
   - It must never put down its weapons or abandon its mission (the warning)

7. **Key Arabic terms preserved:** bāṭil (falsehood), al-khilāfah al-islāmiyyah (Islamic caliphate), istīdādiyyat al-ʿālam (global leadership), ḍalālah (misguidance), naṣr wa tamkīn (victory and establishment), fitnah (trial/tribulation), taraf (luxury), ẓulm (oppression), wilāyah (guardianship/authority of Allah), ḥājah shadīdah wa māssah (severe and agonising need), sarāʾ (prosperity), futūḥāt (conquests), jīl al-taghyīr (generation of change), taghyīr ḥaqīqī (real change).

8. **Hadith source verification:**
   - ʿAbd al-Raḥmān ibn ʿAwf narration: Source is Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī (al-Hilali cites "al-Bukhārī in his Ṣaḥīḥ"). Grade: Ṣaḥīḥ (authentic) by definition.
   - Khabbāb ibn al-Aratt narration: Source is Ḥayāt al-Ṣaḥābah by Muḥammad Yūsuf al-Kāndihlawī 1/100–101, Dār Ṣādir, Beirut. (Footnote 1) The grade is not specified by al-Hilali, but the narration is known and graded ḥasan/ṣaḥīh in hadith literature.

9. **No new Quranic verses in this chunk:** Pages 20-22 reference concepts from verses already cited (24:55, 2:214, etc.) but do not introduce new Quranic quotations. All references are conceptual, not direct citations.
