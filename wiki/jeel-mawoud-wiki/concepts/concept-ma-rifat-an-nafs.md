# المعرفة بالنفس (Ma'rifat an-Nafs) — Knowledge of Self

## Definition

المعرفة بالنفس (ma'rifat an-nafs) — "knowledge of the self/soul" — refers to the deep, certain recognition of one's true nature: one's humble origin, essential weakness, complete dependence on Allah, poverty before Him, and the self's inherent inclination toward desires (shahawāt), transgression (ṭughyān), and self-aggrandizement. This knowledge produces humility (tawāḍu'), caution against the self (ḥadhr min an-nafs), sincere reliance on Allah (tawakkul), and dedication to Him (ikhlāṣ).

The term نفس (nafs) from ن-ف-س (na-fa-sa) carries the primary meaning of "self, soul, breath." In Quranic usage, an-nafs encompasses the entire human self with its desires, inclinations, and capacity for both good and evil. An-nafs al-ammārah bi-sū' (the self commanding evil) is its default state; an-nafs al-lawwāmah (the reproaching self) is the self becoming aware of its defects; an-nafs al-muṭma'innah (the tranquil self) is the self that has been purified.

Al-Hilali positions ma'rifat an-nafs as the **second pivot** (al-miḥwār ath-thānī) of the five knowledges, directly linked to the first: "Every time the servant increases in knowledge of his Lord, his self becomes smaller in his estimation; and every time he forgets his Lord, his forgetfulness of the danger of his self upon him increases" (p. 106).

## Al-Hilali's Treatment

### The Same Principle Applied Inwardly (Ch. 3, pp. 105–106)

Al-Hilali extends the governing principle — "treatment is proportionate to knowledge" — from our relationship with Allah to our relationship with ourselves: "Just as the rule 'treatment is proportionate to knowledge' applies to our relationship with Allah and our way of dealing with Him, it also applies to our view of ourselves and our way of dealing with ourselves" (p. 105).

### The Disease of Self-Admiration (pp. 105–106)

He observes: "One notices that some people deal with themselves with admiration (i'jāb) and satisfaction (riḍā), and they feel their superiority and distinction over others" (p. 105). This incorrect treatment "stems from ignorance of its reality" (p. 105). If a person truly knew his origin, his weakness, his need for his Lord, the severity of his poverty to Him — if this knowledge were deeply rooted in his certainty and interwoven with his īmān — "he would increase his caution against himself, his fear of it, and his sense of need for Allah" (p. 106).

### Knowledge of Self Produces Key Virtues (p. 105–106)

Al-Hilali explicitly identifies the fruits of ma'rifat an-nafs:

1. **Ikhlāṣ (sincerity):** Recognizing one's inability to accomplish anything independently makes one attribute all good to Allah.

2. **Tawakkul (true reliance):** Recognizing one's utter dependence on Allah makes one rely on Him completely.

3. **Tawāḍu' (humility):** "The origin of humility is the servant's considering himself small, seeing himself with the eye of deficiency, and seeing others as better than him" (p. 105).

### The Inverse Relationship: Knowing Allah and Knowing Self (p. 106)

A profound insight: "Knowledge of the self is the second pivot from which the attributes of the promised generation launch. This pivot is undoubtedly closely linked to the pivot of knowledge of Allah. The more the servant increases in knowledge of his Lord, his self becomes smaller in his estimation; and every time he forgets his Lord, his forgetfulness of the danger of his self upon him increases, and then his leaning toward it increases" (p. 106).

He supports this with al-Ḥashr 59:19: "And do not be like those who forgot Allah, so He caused them to forget themselves." Forgetting Allah leads to forgetting one's true nature — and self-forgetfulness is not the healthy kind but the dangerous kind: forgetting that the self is weak, inclined to evil, and in constant need of Allah's guidance.

### The Quran on the Self's Nature (implicit throughout)

While al-Hilali does not cite these verses directly in this section, his argument is consistent with the Quran's description of the nafs:

- "Indeed, the self commands evil" (Yūsuf 12:53) — the default inclination
- "Man was created weak" (an-Nisā' 4:28) — essential fragility
- "And you do not will except that Allah wills" (al-Insān 76:30, at-Takwīr 81:29) — complete dependence

### In Chapter 4: The Quran Reveals the Self (p. 147)

In the context of the Quran as methodology of change, al-Hilali writes: "The Quran reveals to man the truth of his humble origin, and the extent of his weakness and inability, and his ignorance and the size of his needs required for survival, and that he is by Allah not by himself, and if He leaves him for the blink of an eye he perishes" (p. 147). The Quran also "informs him of the nature of his self — its love of desires, its inclination toward transgression and tyranny, commanding evil — so he increases his caution against it" (p. 147).

### In Chapter 5: Tazkiyah as Self-Purification (pp. 183–184)

Al-Hilali identifies tazkiyat an-nafs as the second of the three formation pivots for the promised generation: "Purification of the self with the inherent striving against it (al-mujāhadah), and removal of any manifestations of its self-inflation, which if left would become like an idol that the person associates with his Lord in worship and in seeking help" (p. 183). He warns: "Īmān alone is not sufficient; it must be accompanied by striving against the self for sincerity and truthfulness. Otherwise, the self takes over good deeds and diverts their course from sincerity to Allah to serving its own interests and magnifying its own status" (pp. 183–184).

## Hadith & Sahabah Examples

- **'Abdullāh ibn Mas'ūd and the scroll (pp. 149–150):** When two Tābi'īn brought him a scroll containing "beautiful words" from the People of the Book, he had a basin of water brought and began washing it off with his hand, reciting: "We relate to you the best of stories" (Yūsuf 12:3). Then he said: "These hearts are vessels — fill them with the Quran and do not fill them with anything else." This shows the Sahabah's understanding that the self must be occupied with the Quran to prevent other influences from taking root.

- **The Ansar's humility despite their generosity (p. 111):** Despite their extraordinary īthār (preferring others over themselves) — which "the world had never seen the like of" — the Ansar did not become self-admiring. Their knowledge of Allah kept their knowledge of self in proper proportion.
