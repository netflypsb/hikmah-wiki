# القاعدة الصلبة (al-Qā'idah aṣ-Ṣalbah) — Solid Foundation

## Definition

القاعدة الصلبة (al-qā'idah aṣ-ṣalbah) — "the solid foundation" — is al-Hilali's term for the five knowledges (al-ma'ārif al-khams) that constitute the essential internal structure from which the ten attributes of the promised generation grow. The term qā'idah derives from ق-ع-د (qa-'a-da), meaning "to sit, to be established," and in this context means "base, foundation, fundamental principle." Ṣalbah (feminine of ṣālib) derives from ص-ل-ب (ṣa-la-ba), meaning "solid, firm, hard, strong."

The concept addresses a central problem: many Muslims desire the attributes of the promised generation — ikhlāṣ, love of Allah, fear of Him, zuhd, tawāḍu', jihād, ṣabr — but cannot sustain them. They "read the reports of the righteous and are affected by them and dream of being like them, but cannot transform the dream into reality" (p. 98). The reason: "We want to jump to the fruit directly and do not begin the matter from its beginning" (p. 98). The five knowledges are the roots from which the fruits grow; without them, the attributes are artificial and unsustainable.

The governing principle (al-qā'idah al-ḥākimah) is: **al-mu'āmalah bi-qadr al-ma'rifah** — "Treatment is proportionate to knowledge" (p. 99). The depth and quality of one's relationship with Allah, with oneself, with the world, and with the Hereafter directly correspond to the depth and quality of one's knowledge of these realities.

## Al-Hilali's Treatment

### The Problem: Jumping to the Fruit (Ch. 3, pp. 98–99)

Al-Hilali opens Chapter 3 with a diagnosis: "When we consider the ten attributes that form the minimum for what the promised generation should be upon, we find that achieving them is extremely difficult. If one of us tries to take on some of them for a period, he quickly returns to his former state. The reality is the best proof of that — many of us wish to be ascetic in the world, desiring what is with Allah, longing for Paradise, sincere to Allah, fearing Him, loving Him… but he cannot" (p. 98).

The cause: "We want to jump to the fruit directly and do not begin the matter from its beginning. Since the fruiting stage is the last of the stages of growth, preceded by many stages of care and attention, likewise the ten attributes must — in order to appear — have work and building and internal change that ascends gradually until the effects appear and the fruits ripen" (pp. 98–99).

### The Governing Principle: Treatment Proportionate to Knowledge (Ch. 3, pp. 99–100)

Al-Hilali illustrates with a powerful analogy: two strangers meet on a train. Initially their conversation is guarded; as they get to know each other, the formality dissolves and treatment improves. If one discovers the other holds an important position, his view changes and his treatment reflects the new knowledge. "When we look at our treatment and our relationship with Allah, we find it dry and limited. What governs it and influences it is the degree of our knowledge of Him" (p. 99).

He cites al-An'ām 6:91: "They did not appraise Allah with true appraisal." And: "The more the servants' ignorance of Allah increases, the more their incorrect conception of Him increases, and that is reflected in the form of their treatment with Him" (p. 99). He cites al-Baqarah 2:142: "The foolish among the people will say, 'What has turned them away from their qiblah?' — they are called 'foolish' (sufahā') because they do not know Allah and do not know He owns the heavens and the earth and does what He wills" (p. 100).

### The First Pivot: Knowledge of Allah (Ch. 3, pp. 102–105)

"Treating Allah with ikhlāṣ, love, fear, awe, reverence, taqwa, and surrender — all of that is a product of the strength of knowledge of Him" (p. 102). "The first pivot from which the attributes of the promised generation must launch is knowledge of Allah" (p. 102). The required knowledge must be: qawiyyah (strong), rāsikhah (rooted), 'amīqah (deep), and musttamirrah (continuous) (p. 102). It must "settle in the human's certainty and his inner mind, and affect his feelings, and form an essential part of his faith" (p. 102).

Al-Hilali explains the mechanism: īmān and hawā (desire) compete for control of the heart. "On the measure of the strength of one of them at the moment of decision, the action will be from its share" (p. 103). The Prophet ﷺ said: "The servant does not commit zina while he is a believer, nor does he steal while he is a believer" (al-Bukhārī and Aḥmad from Ibn 'Abbās) — meaning īmān was overcome by hawā at those moments. To increase īmān is to increase the heart's orientation toward Allah, which increases love, fear, awe, and so on (p. 103).

### The Second Pivot: Knowledge of Self (Ch. 3, pp. 105–106)

"Just as the principle 'treatment is proportionate to knowledge' applies to our relationship with Allah, it also applies to our view of ourselves and our way of dealing with ourselves" (p. 105). The mistake: some people "deal with themselves with admiration and satisfaction, and feel their superiority and distinction over others. This incorrect treatment with the self arises from ignorance of its reality" (p. 105). If one truly knew "the truth of his origin, the extent of his weakness, his need for his Lord, the intensity of his poverty to Him" — he would "intensify his caution against his self, his fear of it, and his feeling of need for Allah" (p. 106).

"Knowledge of the self, real knowledge, will lead to more ikhlāṣ and true tawakkul, and will also produce the attribute of tawāḍu' (humility). For the root of tawāḍu' is the servant's considering himself small, seeing it with the eye of deficiency, and seeing others as better than him" (p. 106).

Al-Ḥashr 59:19: "Do not be like those who forgot Allah, so He caused them to forget themselves." The more one knows his Lord, the smaller his self becomes before Him; the more he forgets his Lord, the more he forgets his self's danger upon him (p. 106).

### The Third Pivot: Knowledge of the World (Ch. 3, pp. 106–108)

"What makes us long for the world, and increase our thinking about our future? What causes our greed for wealth, and joy at its increase, and sorrow at its decrease? What makes us extend our eyes to those who have surpassed us in provision and wish to be like them?" (p. 106). The answer: "Ignorance of the world and its reality — that it is merely a station for a traveler's rest, a stage in the journey of return to Allah" (p. 107). And ignorance of "the nature of its adornment and its false reality" (p. 107).

Al-Hilali uses an analogy: if someone invites you to spend summer in a country, and you discover it has extreme heat, constant power cuts, rampant disease, high humidity, and thieves on the roads — "what will be your reaction? Without doubt you will lose desire to go there." The world is the same: "The main reason for our attachment to it is a result of our ignorance of its reality" (p. 107). "Knowledge of the world is the third pivot from which the attributes of the promised generation launch, especially the attribute of zuhd" (p. 108).

### The Fourth Pivot: Faith in the Hereafter (Ch. 3, pp. 108–109)

"In contrast to our dealing with the world, we deal with the Hereafter. Despite the Hereafter being the abode of permanence, and despite Paradise containing forms of bliss that the limited human mind cannot comprehend, and despite the Fire containing forms of punishment that no human can bear — we do not deal with this reality as we should. We are not longing for Paradise, rather we are ascetic toward it; we are not fearful of the Fire, rather we are not working to escape it. This is undoubtedly a result of ignorance of their reality" (p. 108).

"Clarity of vision regarding Paradise, and the absence of the reality of the Hereafter from the Muslim's mind, has a great role in longing for it — which reflects on one's actions: hastening in good deeds, increasing worship, striving in Allah's cause, and patience on the hardships of the path" (p. 109). "Knowledge of the Hereafter and faith in it is the fourth pivot from which the attributes of the promised generation launch" (p. 109).

### The Fifth Pivot: Correct Understanding of Islam (Ch. 3, pp. 109–111)

The attribute of i'tidāl (moderation) and tawāzun (balance) — the tenth attribute — "is a fruit of the correct understanding of the religion and its comprehensiveness, and the ranks of its rulings" (p. 110). It is also "a fruit of knowledge of fiqh al-awlawiyyāt (understanding priorities), fiqh al-mawāzīnāt (understanding balances), fiqh al-wāqi' (understanding reality), fiqh al-maqāṣid (understanding objectives), and fiqh al-khilāf (understanding disagreement)" (p. 110).

This fifth pivot is a regulator (ḍābiṭ): "It is expected that these four knowledges, when they take hold of the servant, will push him to leave the world or be severe upon himself and deprive it of some of its permissible pleasures, and may push him to strictness where strictness is not warranted, and leniency where leniency is not warranted" (p. 110). Al-ḥikmah, al-i'tidāl, al-tawāzun, and al-wasṭiyyah are "good fruits of the correct understanding of the religion" (p. 110). "Correct understanding of Islam is the fifth pivot from which the attributes of the promised generation launch" (p. 110).

### The Three Formation Pivots (Ch. 5, pp. 183–185)

Al-Hilali summarizes the formation process as three practical pivots for the promised generation:

1. **Deep īmān in Allah and the Last Day** — the product of the first and fourth knowledges (p. 183)
2. **Tazkiyah of the self** — the product of the second knowledge; jihād against the self for truthfulness and ikhlāṣ (pp. 184–185)
3. **Correct understanding of Islam** — the fifth knowledge, with its comprehensiveness, balance, and understanding of priorities, objectives, and reality (p. 185)

"The fundamental starting point for these three pivots must be the Quran" (p. 185).

## Hadith & Sahabah Examples

- **The Prophet ﷺ on īmān and hawā (p. 103):** "The servant does not commit zina while he is a believer, nor does he steal while he is a believer, nor does he drink alcohol while he is a believer, nor does he kill while he is a believer." (Al-Bukhārī and Aḥmad from Ibn 'Abbās) — moments of sin represent the triumph of hawā over īmān.

- **The Prophet ﷺ on complete īmān (p. 104):** "Whoever loves Allah and hates for Allah and gives for Allah and withholds for Allah has completed īmān." (Abū Dāwūd) — complete orientation of emotions toward Allah.

- **The Prophet's du'ā' (p. 104):** "O Allah, apportion for us from Your fear what will come between us and Your disobedience." (Aḥmad, at-Tirmidhī, al-Ḥākim from Ibn 'Umar; al-Albānī rated it ḥasan, Ṣaḥīḥ al-Jāmi' 1268) — fear as the regulator of behavior.

- **Mūsā's increased knowledge (pp. 101–102):** When Allah revealed Himself to the mountain and it crumbled, Mūsā fell unconscious. When he awoke, his knowledge of Allah's greatness had increased, and he said: "Glory be to You! I repent to You, and I am the first of the believers." (al-A'rāf 7:143) — increased knowledge leading to repentance and improved treatment.

- **The Ansar's īmān (p. 111):** Their īmān penetrated to the point where "it mixed with their flesh and blood" — producing love of the Muhājirūn, i'thār (preferring others over themselves), all despite poverty, not wealth. (al-Ḥashr 59:9)
