# الإيمان بالآخرة (al-Īmān bil Ākhirah) — Faith in the Hereafter

## Definition

الإيمان بالآخرة (al-īmān bil ākhirah) — "faith in the Hereafter" — refers to the deep, certainty-level belief in the existence, nature, and imminence of the afterlife: Paradise (al-Jannah) with its incomprehensible delights, and the Fire (an-Nār) with its unbearable torments. This belief must be so clear and rooted that it continuously motivates the believer toward righteous action, sacrifice, patience, and detachment from the world — producing the urgency (mushāmaharah) for Paradise and the urgency of escape (al-harab) from the Fire.

The term الآخرة (al-ākhirah) from أ-خ-ر ('a-kha-ra) means "the last, the final, the ultimate." It is contrasted with الدنيا (ad-dunyā) — "the lower, the nearer" — from د-ن-و (da-na-wa). The linguistic contrast itself teaches: the Hereafter is the real destination; the world is merely proximate, passing, subordinate.

In al-Hilali's methodology, al-īmān bil ākhirah is the **fourth pivot** (al-miḥwār ar-rābi') of the five knowledges. Together with knowledge of the world (the third pivot), it creates the push-pull dynamic: zuhd (detachment) from the world pushes the believer away, while desire for Paradise pulls him forward.

## Al-Hilali's Treatment

### The Paradox: Believing Without Acting (Ch. 3, pp. 108–109)

Al-Hilali states the paradox directly: "Despite the Hereafter being the abode of eternity (dār al-qarār), and in Paradise there are forms of delight that the limited human mind cannot grasp, and in the Fire there are forms of torment that no human can bear — we do not deal with this reality as we should. We are not yearning for Paradise (mushmirīn); rather, we are ascetic toward it (zāhidīn fīhā) and in everything that brings one close to it. We are not fearful or awed by the Fire, nor working to escape it" (p. 108). The cause: "ignorance of its reality" (al-jahl bi-ḥaqīqatihimā).

### Clear Vision Motivates Action (p. 108)

"The clarity of vision (wuḍūḥ ar-ru'yah) regarding Paradise and the absence of its reality from the Muslim's mind has a great role in yearning for it, which reflects on his actions — from hastening in good deeds, increasing worship, striving in the cause of Allah, and patience on the hardships of the path" (p. 108).

He uses the analogy of the world (developed in the third pivot, p. 107): just as ignorance of a vacation destination's defects makes you eager to go there, ignorance of Paradise's delights makes you indifferent to it. The solution is not merely more information but deeper knowledge that penetrates yaqīn.

### The Fourth Pivot (p. 109)

"Knowledge of the Hereafter and faith in it constitutes the fourth pivot from which the attributes of the promised generation launch" (p. 109). The specific attributes it produces include: mushāmaharah (yearning for Paradise), sa'b (exertion), jihād (striving), taḍḥiyyah (sacrifice), ṣabr (patience), and thabāt (steadfastness).

### In Chapter 5: The Price Is Paradise (pp. 179–181)

The most vivid treatment of al-īmān bil ākhirah comes in Chapter 5, where al-Hilali narrates the pledge of al-'Aqabah. When the Prophet ﷺ sought the Ansar's protection, "he did not promise them anything of the world in exchange… but he made the price Paradise (al-Jannah)" (p. 179). Jaabir ibn 'Abd Allāh narrates: The Prophet ﷺ said: "I take your pledge upon: hearing and obeying in activity and laziness, spending in difficulty and ease, enjoining good and forbidding evil, speaking for Allah fearing no blame, and supporting me when you come to me — and for you is Paradise" (pp. 179–180).

As'ad ibn Zurārah warned them: "If you bring him out today, it means parting with all the Arabs and the killing of your best men." They replied: "By Allah, we will never abandon this pledge, nor will we steal it!" (p. 180). Their īmān bil ākhirah was the basis of their commitment.

The Ansar fulfilled this pledge: "They continued upon their pledge, defending Islam and protecting its realm. They were many at times of fear and few at times of greed. They continued supporting their brothers the Muhājirūn — even preferring them over themselves — all of that longing for the condition upon which they pledged: Paradise" (p. 180).

### The World's Weight vs. Hereafter's Lightness (Ch. 5, pp. 176–183)

Al-Hilali argues that those most qualified for the promised generation's attributes "are those who have not drowned in the sea of the world, and upon whose hearts the heavy weights (al-athqāl) have not been placed, drawing them to the earth and preventing them from freedom of movement" (p. 178). He cites the hadith of the Prophet who went on campaign and set three conditions: no one who has bought a slave woman he wishes to sleep with, nor one who has built a building without completing its roof, nor one who has bought sheep awaiting their birth — "the axis of these conditions is: not being preoccupied (shughl al-bāl) or the heart's attachment to something of the world" (p. 178).

Imam an-Nawawī comments: "Important matters should not be entrusted except to those of determination and free mind, and not to one whose heart is attached to other than them, because that weakens his resolve and detracts from the perfection of his effort" (p. 179).

### Islam's Purchasing of the Believers (p. 181)

Al-Hilali cites at-Tawbah 9:111: "Indeed, Allah has purchased from the believers their lives and their property — for them is Paradise." He concludes: "This is what Islam wants now: a generation that looks to nothing of the wreckage of the world… a generation whose sight is fixed on heaven, constantly striving for Allah's pleasure and His Paradise" (p. 181).

### Not a Call to Monasticism (pp. 182–183)

Al-Hilali explicitly clarifies: "The meaning is not that the one sought from the generation of hope should not marry or work. But the purpose is that he prepares himself for the great role entrusted to him, and feels its importance and that it surpasses anything else" (p. 182). The believer will marry — but will choose a wife who helps his mission. He will work — but in a job that doesn't consume all his time. He will have children — but will raise them as part of the promised generation.

## Hadith & Sahabah Examples

- **The Pledge of al-'Aqabah (pp. 179–180):** The Prophet ﷺ took the Ansar's pledge upon obedience, spending, enjoining good, speaking for Allah, and supporting Islam — with Paradise as the sole compensation. (Reported by al-Bazzār from Jābir)

- **The Prophet's campaign conditions (pp. 178–179):** A Prophet who went on campaign set three conditions: no one with a slave woman he wishes to sleep with but hasn't, no one who built a building without a roof, no one who bought sheep awaiting birth. The axis: hearts not attached to worldly concerns. (al-Bukhārī and Muslim)

- **Imam an-Nawawī's commentary (p. 179):** "Important matters should not be entrusted except to those of determination and free mind, and not to one whose heart is attached to other than them." (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim bi-Sharḥ an-Nawawī, 12/279)

- **The Ansar's continued fidelity (p. 180):** After the Pledge, they continued defending Islam, preferring the Muhājirūn over themselves, and dividing their date harvests giving the larger portion to the Muhājirūn. When the Prophet ﷺ later offered to redistribute, they said: "You had a condition upon us, and we had a condition upon you — that for us is Paradise. We have done what you asked — so give us our condition!" (Reported by al-Bazzār)

- **'Abd ar-Raḥmān ibn 'Awf weeping (p. 21):** Remembering the martyrs' austerity while the world was "spread out for us" — fearing their reward had been given in worldly form. This illustrates the Sahabah's constant awareness that the Hereafter, not the world, is the true abode.
