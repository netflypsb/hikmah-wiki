# الجيل الموعود (al-Jīl al-Maw'ūd) — The Promised Generation

## Definition

الجيل الموعود بالنصر والتمكين (al-Jīl al-Maw'ūd bi-an-Naṣr wa at-Tamkīn) — "The Generation Promised Victory and Empowerment" — is the central concept of Dr. Majdi al-Hilali's book. It refers to a future generation of Muslims who will fulfil the conditions for divine victory (an-naṣr) and earthly empowerment (at-tamkīn), thereby restoring the ummah to its rightful position of leadership and honour.

The Arabic term الجيل (al-jīl) derives from the root ج-و-ل (j-w-l), meaning "to circulate, to pass through a period," and denotes a cohort of people living in the same era. الموعود (al-maw'ūd) is the passive participle from و-ع-د (wa'ada), meaning "promised" — specifically, promised by Allah through His revealed verses. Thus, al-Jīl al-Maw'ūd is not a theoretical construct but a Quranically-grounded promise: Allah has vowed that when a community fulfills the conditions of faith, He will grant them sovereignty and establish His religion on earth.

The concept bridges two realities: the current state of the ummah — characterised by weakness (ḍu'f), humiliation (dhull), and fragmentation (tafarruq) — and the prophesied future state of victory and empowerment. The generation that will carry the ummah from one state to the other is al-Jīl al-Maw'ūd. The full title of al-Hilali's work makes this explicit: *al-Jīl al-Maw'ūd bi-an-Naṣr wa at-Tamkīn* — the generation whose arrival is promised by Allah's own word, contingent upon the fulfillment of the conditions embedded in the Quranic promise.

## Al-Hilali's Treatment

### The Ummah's Crisis (Ch. 1, pp. 10–11)

Al-Hilali opens by diagnosing the ummah's condition with stark language: "What is happening to our Islamic ummah of humiliation and disgrace is hidden from no one. Wounds fill the body of the ummah, and weakness and frailty have struck it" (p. 10). The Muslims are "the most humiliated people on earth," with "no value and no consideration for our existence" (p. 10). He describes the enemies' campaigns — "the monkeys and pigs and their allies who placed us under their feet and made us taste the worst punishment" — killing children, demolishing homes, and imprisoning youth, all under the world's watch (p. 10).

The material reality confirms continued decline: "The material and technological superiority that our enemies have achieved makes it difficult for us to catch up with them" and "they will not allow us to approach them" (p. 11). The "calculations of the people of the earth" (ḥisābāt ahl al-arḍ) confirm the ummah's continued weakness (p. 11).

### Hope in Allah Alone (Ch. 1, pp. 11–12)

Against this bleak picture, al-Hilali insists that hope lies solely in divine power (al-quwwah al-ilāhiyyah), not in material calculations. The "calculations of the people of faith" (ḥisābāt ahl al-īmān) reveal "a tremendous power that the human mind cannot comprehend the extent of" (p. 11). He cites the example of the weak among Banī Isrā'īl who were supported against Pharaoh: "And We caused the people who had been oppressed to inherit the eastern and western portions of the land that We blessed" (al-A'rāf 7:137, p. 12).

Yet he immediately clarifies: this does not mean abandoning material causes (al-asbāb). Rather, "the required is the opposite — that we fill every gap available to us, and penetrate all sectors, and exert the utmost effort (ghāyat al-ijtihād) in possessing the causes of power" (p. 12). The command to prepare is explicit: "And prepare against them whatever you are able of power" (al-Anfāl 8:60, p. 12). But causes alone do not produce victory: "On the measure of our effort in taking what is available to us of causes, we will have fulfilled a condition of the conditions of naṣr and change, with the understanding that the causes by themselves will not bring us naṣr, but rather what will bring it is Allah" (p. 12).

### Conditions for Victory (Ch. 1, pp. 13–14)

Victory and empowerment have conditions (shurūṭ) that must be met. Three key verses establish this:

1. **Internal change:** "Indeed, Allah does not change the condition of a people until they change what is within themselves" (al-Ra'd 13:11, p. 13)
2. **Supporting Allah:** "If you support Allah, He will support you and plant firmly your feet" (Muḥammad 47:7, p. 13)
3. **Patience and taqwa:** "If you are patient and fear Allah, their plot will not harm you at all" (Āl 'Imrān 3:120, p. 13)

Al-Hilali concludes: "There must be a support for Allah upon ourselves, and a change of what is in them, and a transition from every state that angers Allah to the state that pleases Him" (p. 13). Deep change (taghyīr 'amīq) is required — a change that strikes into the depths of the ummah and returns it to Allah.

### The Need for the Generation (Ch. 1, pp. 14–22)

Al-Hilali argues that for the ummah to change from sickness to health, there must be someone who takes it by the hand: "There must be a group (ṭā'ifah) or a generation (jīl) that launches into the midst of the ummah, reviving the spirit and hope, and showing it the path of reconciliation with Allah" (p. 14). This is al-Jīl al-Maw'ūd. He outlines four phases of the generation's role:

**1. Breathing life and directing sentiment (pp. 14–15):** The ummah already possesses religious sentiment (al-'āṭifah) — manifested in filled mosques, Qur'an study circles, and the spread of hijab — but this sentiment alone is insufficient. It must be directed toward "genuine change that reshapes the intellect, anchors faith in the heart, expels love of the world, and accelerates good deeds" (pp. 14–15). Especially if the generation adopts "the method of wisdom in da'wah, and presents the concept of change to people in a loved and simple way that everyone can understand" (p. 15).

**2. Leadership in hardship (al-ḍarrā', pp. 16–18):** When the rising ummah faces the inevitable boycott, siege, and pressures from enemies, the generation will "work to provide people's needs, spread hope, and bind them to Allah" (p. 17). He notes that "these devoted ones will not sleep in these circumstances except after the people have slept, and will not eat except after they are assured that food has reached everyone" (p. 17).

**3. Leadership in prosperity (al-sarrā', pp. 20–22):** After the breakthrough, the generation will work to "unite the ummah under one banner, lead it to establish the Islamic caliphate, recover every occupied land, and rescue the oppressed Muslims" (p. 20). It will continue spreading Islam until "the Islamic state becomes the first power in the world, spreading security and safety" (p. 20).

**4. Continuity after empowerment (pp. 21–22):** The generation must not rest after achieving sovereignty. It must "continue building generations, guarding against the diseases of nations — luxury (taraf), oppression (ẓulm), and distance from Allah" (p. 22). "He will not lay down his weapons or lean to comfort after that, but will continue building generations and preserving the glory and honour and working to spread the true religion in every place" (p. 22).

### The Five Pillars of the Generation's Character (Ch. 3, pp. 98–112)

Chapter 3 reveals the methodology: the attributes of al-Jīl al-Maw'ūd are natural fruits (thamar ṭabī'ī) of five knowledges (ma'ārif). The governing principle is: "Treatment is proportionate to knowledge" (al-mu'āmalah bi-qadr al-ma'rifah, p. 99). The five pillars are:

1. **Knowledge of Allah (Ma'rifat Allāh)** — the first and most important pivot (pp. 102–105)
2. **Knowledge of Self (Ma'rifat an-Nafs)** — the second pivot (pp. 105–106)
3. **Knowledge of the World (Ma'rifat ad-Dunyā)** — the third pivot, especially for the attribute of zuhd (pp. 106–108)
4. **Knowledge of the Hereafter (al-Īmān bil Ākhirah)** — the fourth pivot (pp. 108–109)
5. **Correct Understanding of Islam (al-Fahm aṣ-Ṣaḥīḥ lid-Dīn)** — the fifth pivot, producing wisdom, moderation, and balance; it acts as a regulator (ḍābiṭ) preventing extremism (pp. 109–111)

These five knowledges "must take root in the Muslim's certainty and affect his feelings and attract his faith, so that the attributes that Allah wants from His servants will grow naturally (bi-ṣūrah talqā'iyyah) and continuously" (p. 112).

### Not Scattered Individuals but a Bonded Generation (Ch. 5, pp. 176–178)

Al-Hilali stresses that the promised generation is not "scattered individuals here and there who do not feel one another" but rather "a bonded, cohesive generation (jīl mutarābiṭ mutamāsik muta'ākhin) that moves with measured steps, each individual standing at a gap (thaghr) of the Islamic ummah's gaps — waking the sleepers, guiding the awake, and uniting efforts" (p. 177). Coordination and mutual understanding around the Islamic project are essential.

### The Need for Freedom from Worldly Weights (Ch. 5, pp. 178–182)

Those most qualified for the promised generation's attributes "are those who have not drowned in the sea of the world, and upon whose hearts the heavy weights (al-athqāl) have not been placed, drawing them to the earth and preventing them from freedom of movement" (p. 178). He cites the Prophet's campaign conditions: no one with a slave woman he wishes to sleep with, no one who built a building without a roof, no one who bought sheep awaiting birth — "the axis of these conditions is: not being preoccupied (shughl al-bāl) or the heart's attachment to something of the world" (pp. 178–179).

Youth are particularly suited: "As for the youth, since they have fewer weights, they are without doubt more ready to complete the attributes of the promised generation, with what distinguishes them as well of surging emotion, fierce resolve, leadership, and lofty aspiration" (pp. 181–182). But older people can also qualify "if we resolve, purify our intention, lighten our weights, and make Allah alone our goal" (p. 182).

### Not a Call to Monasticism (Ch. 5, pp. 182–183)

Al-Hilali explicitly clarifies: "The meaning is not that the one sought from the generation of hope should not marry or work. But the purpose is that he prepares himself for the great role entrusted to him, and feels its importance and that it surpasses anything else" (p. 182). He will marry but choose a wife who helps his mission; he will work but in a job that doesn't consume all his time; he will have children but raise them as part of the promised generation.

## Hadith & Sahabah Examples

- **Abu al-'Āliyah's commentary on An-Nūr 24:55 (p. 18):** The Prophet ﷺ and his companions were in Mecca for about ten years calling to Allah in secret, afraid, not commanded to fight. Then they migrated and were commanded to fight while still afraid — wearing arms morning and evening. A companion asked: "Will we remain afraid like this forever?" The Prophet ﷺ said: "It will not be long until a man sits in the great gathering with no weapon." Then the verse was revealed and Allah manifested His Prophet over the Arabian Peninsula.

- **'Abd ar-Raḥmān ibn 'Awf weeping (p. 21):** When brought food while fasting, he said: "Muṣ'ab ibn 'Umayr was killed and he was better than me, and was shrouded in a single garment… and Ḥamzah was killed and he was better than me… then the world was spread out for us." He wept and left the food — fearing their good deeds had been hastened. (Reported by al-Bukhārī)

- **Khabbāb ibn al-Arat weeping (p. 21):** When visiting the graves of Uhud martyrs, companions would say: "Good news, O servant of Allah! Your brothers will be hosted tomorrow." He wept: "It is not from fear, but you have reminded me of people whose rewards went with them as they were, and I fear that our reward for those deeds you mention has been given to us after them." (Ḥayāt aṣ-Ṣaḥābah, 1/100–101)

- **The Ansar's pledge at al-'Aqabah (pp. 179–181):** When the Prophet ﷺ asked for their protection, he offered only Paradise — not any worldly gain. As'ad ibn Zurārah warned them: "If you bring him out today, it means parting with all the Arabs and the killing of your best men." They replied: "By Allah, we will never abandon this pledge, nor will we steal it!" Their price was Paradise alone. (Reported by al-Bazzār from Jābir)

- **The Prophet's campaign conditions (pp. 178–179):** A Prophet who went on campaign set three conditions excluding those whose hearts were attached to worldly concerns. Imam an-Nawawī comments: "Important matters should not be entrusted except to those of determination and free mind, and not to one whose heart is attached to other than them, because that weakens his resolve and detracts from the perfection of his effort." (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim bi-Sharḥ an-Nawawī, 12/279)
