# الأخذ بالأسباب (al-Akhdhu bil Asbāb) — Taking All Necessary Causes

## Definition

الأخذ بالأسباب (al-akhdhu bil asbāb) — "taking hold of the causes" — is the Islamic principle that requires believers to exert maximum effort in utilising all available material and immaterial means (asbāb) toward achieving their goals, while simultaneously recognising that these means by themselves do not produce results — only Allah grants success. The root س-ب-ب (sa-ba-ba) means "to cause, to be the cause of," and asbāb (plural of sabab) refers to the means, causes, or channels through which effects come about.

The concept resolves a critical tension in Islamic thought: between tawakkul (reliance upon Allah) and the command to take action. Neglecting causes is not tawakkul but tawākul (lazy reliance); relying on causes as if they inherently produce results is shirk in al-rubūbiyyah (associating partners with Allah in lordship). The correct position is to "take the causes as if everything depends on them, and rely on Allah as if nothing depends on them" — or as al-Hilali articulates it, to exert "the utmost effort in possessing the causes of power, not because we will be victorious through them, but because Allah has required that of us" (p. 12).

In al-Hilali's methodology, al-akhdhu bil asbāb is essential for the promised generation's mission: the ummah must fill every gap, penetrate every sector, and exhaust every available means — while understanding that naṣr comes only from Allah.

## Al-Hilali's Treatment

### The Command to Take Causes (Ch. 1, pp. 11–12)

Al-Hilali confronts the misconception that hope in Allah means abandoning material effort: "It is not the meaning of saying our hope is in Allah alone that we abandon material causes with the claim of their uselessness, and that our enemies will not allow us to compete with them or approach them in the fields of scientific progress" (p. 12). Instead: "The required is the opposite — that we fill every gap available to us (namlā' kulla farāgh yutāḥ amāmanā), and penetrate all sectors (nanataghghal fī kull al-qiṭā'āt), and exert the utmost effort in possessing the causes of power (najtahid ghāyat al-ijtihād fī imtilāk asbāb al-quwwah)" (p. 12).

The command is explicit in al-Anfāl 8:60: "And prepare against them whatever you are able of power and of steeds of war by which you may terrify the enemy of Allah and your enemy" (p. 12). Preparation is not optional; it is a divine command. "On the measure of our effort in taking what is available to us of causes, we will have fulfilled a condition (sharṭ) of the conditions of naṣr and change" (p. 12).

### Causes Alone Do Not Produce Naṣr (Ch. 1, p. 12)

Having commanded the taking of causes, al-Hilali immediately warns against placing trust in them: "With the understanding that the causes by themselves will not bring us naṣr, but what will bring it is Allah" (p. 12). He cites two decisive verses:

- **al-Anfāl 8:10:** "Victory is only from Allah" (wa-mā an-naṣru illā min 'ind Allāh)
- **Āl 'Imrān 3:160:** "If Allah helps you, none can overcome you; and if He forsakes you, who is there that can help you after Him? And upon Allah let the believers rely" (p. 12)

This dual teaching — take causes fully, but trust in Allah alone — is the essence of the Islamic position on asbāb.

### The Example of Banī Isrā'īl (Ch. 1, p. 12)

The Quranic precedent is al-A'rāf 7:137: "And We caused the people who had been oppressed to inherit the eastern and western portions of the land that We blessed." The "weak group" (al-fa'ah aḍ-ḍa'īfah) was supported by divine power, not by their own material superiority. Yet they had to act — they had to leave Egypt, follow Mūsā, cross the sea. Their taking of causes was part of fulfilling the conditions.

### Taking Causes as a Condition, Not a Guarantee (Ch. 1, pp. 12–13)

Al-Hilali's formulation is precise: taking causes is a *condition* (sharṭ) of naṣr, not its *cause* ('illah) in the philosophical sense. A condition must be met, but meeting it does not automatically produce the result — the result comes from Allah's will. This preserves both human responsibility (taking causes is mandatory) and divine sovereignty (results come only from Allah).

### Asbāb and the Sahabah at Tabuk (Ch. 5, p. 176)

Allah severely rebuked the Sahabah who hesitated at Tabūk: "O you who believe, what is with you that when it is said to you, 'Go forth in the cause of Allah,' you cling heavily to the earth? Are you satisfied with the life of this world over the Hereafter?" (at-Tawbah 9:38, p. 176). Their attachment to the world — the dates and the shade — prevented them from taking the necessary cause of going forth. Al-Hilali warns: "If this rebuke was for the Sahabah with their great precedence in supporting Islam, then what if the Quran were revealed upon us now? What would Allah say to us in it?" (p. 176).

### The Formative Period and Asbāb (Ch. 5, pp. 183–185)

Al-Hilali explains that the Quran must be the "fundamental starting point" (munṭaliq asāsī) for the promised generation's formation, and all other means serve this: "Yes, there is no harm in using any means that serves the three formation pivots, but its starting point must be the Quran" (p. 185). The Sīrah Nabawiyyah, Islamic history, and the Sunnah are means — but they must be connected to the Quran and studied as practical applications of it (p. 185).

### Asbāb and the Current Reality (Ch. 5, pp. 176–177)

The ummah's current failure is not merely in taking insufficient causes, but in misplacing its priorities: "We want to serve our religion but while preserving our worldly gains in it" (p. 176). The correct approach: "There must be clarity of vision regarding the nature and role of this generation, and that it is not like other people in its dreams and aspirations" (p. 177). Taking the right causes requires correct understanding — which is itself one of the five knowledges.

### The Five Knowledges as Asbāb (Ch. 3, pp. 98–112)

The five knowledges (ma'rifat Allāh, ma'rifat an-nafs, ma'rifat ad-dunyā, al-īmān bil ākhirah, al-fahm aṣ-ṣaḥīḥ) are themselves the most important asbāb for the formation of the promised generation's attributes. They are the internal causes that produce the external fruits. Without them, "the required is the opposite… that we fill every gap available to us" (p. 12) becomes mere activism without spiritual substance.

### Ḥikmah as the Regulator of Asbāb (Ch. 3, pp. 109–111)

Correct understanding of Islam (the fifth knowledge) regulates the taking of causes: "It is expected that these four knowledges, when they take hold of the servant, will push him to abandon the world or be severe upon himself and deprive it of some of its permissible pleasures, and may push him to strictness where strictness is not warranted, and leniency where leniency is not warranted. From here, ḥikmah and i'tidāl and tawāzun were the good fruits of the correct understanding of the religion" (p. 110). Without this regulator, taking causes can become either excessive asceticism or reckless activism.

## Hadith & Sahabah Examples

- **The Prophet's campaign conditions (pp. 178–179):** A Prophet set conditions for those who would join his campaign — excluding anyone whose heart was attached to worldly concerns (a slave woman, an unfinished building, sheep awaiting birth). Imam an-Nawawī: "Important matters should not be entrusted except to those of determination and free mind, and not to one whose heart is attached to other than them, because that weakens his resolve and detracts from the perfection of his effort." The taking of the cause (joining the campaign) required freedom from competing attachments.

- **The Ansar at al-'Aqabah (pp. 179–181):** They took the ultimate cause — pledging allegiance — and offered only Paradise as the price. Their worldly calculations were set aside; the cause they took was spiritual, not material.

- **The Sahabah at Tabūk (p. 176):** Those who hesitated failed to take the required cause. Allah's rebuke was severe — and al-Hilali asks what the rebuke would be for us today.

- **Muṣ'ab ibn 'Umayr in Yathrib (p. 152):** He was sent with "nothing but the Quran" and the city was "opened by the Quran" before the Prophet's arrival. The cause taken was exclusively Quranic engagement — and it produced extraordinary results.
