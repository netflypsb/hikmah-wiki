# Chapter 5: Where Are We from the Promised Generation? (أين نحن من الجيل الموعود؟)

## Summary

This chapter poses the searching question: where does the contemporary Muslim community stand relative to the attributes of the promised generation? After surveying the attributes (Ch. 2), the knowledge axes that produce them (Ch. 3), and the model of the first generation (Ch. 4), the author now demands honest self-assessment. He acknowledges that we possess some positives — īmān and love of Allah in our hearts — but also many negatives: we love the dunya, plan for our worldly future more than our ākhirah, want to serve religion while keeping our worldly gains. Allah severely rebuked the Ṣaḥābah at Tabūk for reluctance due to heat and ripe fruit — how much more severe would the Qur'an's reproach be for us today? The author then lays out the practical path forward: a serious stand with ourselves, reordering priorities, forming the generation through three axes (deep īmān, tazkiyah of the nafs, proper understanding of Islam), all launched from a genuine return to the Qur'an. He stresses that the promised generation is not scattered individuals but a bonded, coordinated body. He argues that shedding worldly burdens — especially before being weighed down by family and career — is essential, and that youth are best positioned for this. This is not a call for monasticism (rahhāniyyah) but for preparing oneself for the great role of awakening the ummah.

## Key Arguments

- We claim to want to serve religion while preserving our worldly gains — but this is incompatible with being the promised generation; divine aid comes only to those who fulfil the conditions (p. 176)
- Allah rebuked the Ṣaḥābah at Tabūk for reluctance caused by heat and ripe fruits (9:38–39); if this was their rebuke despite their great precedence, what would the Qur'an say to us? (pp. 176–177)
- An honest stand with ourselves, reordering priorities, and defining our goal with sincerity is the essential starting point (p. 177)
- The promised generation is not scattered individuals but a bonded, coordinated body (jīl mutrābiṭ mutākhin) that moves with balanced steps (pp. 177–178)
- Those most qualified to complete the attributes are those who have not drowned in the dunya and whose hearts have not been weighed down by its burdens (p. 178)
- The hadith of the prophet who set conditions for his army — no one attached to worldly concerns should march — illustrates the principle of being free from worldly attachments for serious duty (pp. 178–179)
- The Pledge of al-'Aqabah: the Prophet ﷺ offered only Jannah in exchange for protection and support, refusing any worldly incentive — the price of Jannah (pp. 179–181)
- The Anṣār kept their pledge and continued giving even when Khaybar's wealth arrived — they did not seek the worldly portion (p. 181)
- Allah's transaction: {إِنَّ اللَّهَ اشْتَرَى مِنَ الْمُؤْمِنِينَ أَنْفُسَهُمْ وَأَمْوَالَهُمْ بِأَنَّ لَهُمُ الْجَنَّةَ} (9:111) (p. 181)
- Youth are best positioned for the promised generation due to fewer worldly burdens, combined with their energy, enthusiasm, and high resolve — but older people can also qualify if they resolve, purify intention, lighten their loads, and make Allah their only goal (pp. 181–182)
- This is not a call for monasticism — one may marry, work, and have children, but the guiding purpose is the ummah's awakening, not mere worldly accumulation (pp. 182–183)
- Three axes of formation (muhāwirat al-takwīn): (1) deep īmān in Allah and the ākhirah, (2) tazkiyah of the nafs from shuhh and hidden self-worship, (3) proper understanding of Islam with balance, priorities, and awareness of reality (pp. 183–185)
- All three axes must be launched from the Qur'an as the primary source; the sīrah and Islamic history supplement but must be connected to the Qur'an (pp. 185–186)
- The Qur'an has a unique property — it generates continuous energy, enthusiasm, and positivity in whoever engages with it properly (pp. 186–187)
- "Return to the Qur'an" means returning to its real value as an instrument of guidance, healing, correction, and change — not speed-memorisation programmes (pp. 186–187)
- Practical steps for Qur'anic engagement: daily quiet reading with contemplation, preparation through remembering death and the ākhirah, reading slowly and with emotion, repeating verses that move the heart, memorising in small portions with action preceding further memorisation (pp. 187–188)
- The Qur'an is the project of the ummah's renaissance (mashrū' al-nahḍah) — we must cling to it and call others to it (p. 188)
- Even if alone, one should begin by clinging to the Qur'an and calling those around — family, neighbours, colleagues — and establishing educational gatherings (muḥāḍin tarbawiyyah) like Dār al-Arqam (pp. 189–190)

## Sahabah Examples

- **The Ṣaḥābah at Tabūk**: Rebuked by Allah (9:38–39) for reluctance — despite their great precedence, their attachment to worldly comfort drew severe correction (pp. 176–177)
- **The Anṣār at the Pledge of al-'Aqabah**: Pledged protection and support for Jannah alone; when offered release from their pact after Khaybar's wealth, they refused — "We have a condition: for us is Jannah" (pp. 179–181)
- **Sa'd ibn Zurārah**: The youngest at the Pledge of al-'Aqabah; he warned the crowd about the gravity of what they were pledging (p. 180)
- **'Umar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb**: His conversion story illustrates the importance of educational gatherings — he went to his sister's house and found Khabbāb ibn al-Arat teaching Qur'an there (p. 190)
- **Jābir ibn 'Abd Allāh**: Narrated the account of the Anṣār's pledge and their condition of Jannah (p. 179)
