# Chapter 3: How Are the Attributes Achieved? (كيف تحقق صفات الجيل الموعود؟)

## Summary

This chapter addresses the central practical question of the book: how can the ten attributes of the promised generation — identified in Chapter 2 — be actually realised? The author argues that these attributes (ikhlāṣ, mahabbah, khawf, 'ibādah, tawāḍu', zuhd, jihād, ṣabr, i'tidāl, ukhuwwah) cannot be artificially assumed or externally imposed. Rather, they are natural fruits that grow from five foundational knowledges (ma'ārif): knowledge of Allah (معرفة الله), knowledge of the self (معرفة النفس), knowledge of the dunya (معرفة الدنيا), knowledge of the ākhirah (معرفة الآخرة), and proper understanding of Islam (الفهم الصحيح للدين). The governing principle is "treatment is proportionate to knowledge" (المعاملة بقدر المعرفة) — the deeper and more firmly rooted the knowledge, the more authentic and enduring the resulting attributes. The chapter then details how each of the five ma'ārif is acquired through three primary means: the Qur'an, reflecting on life events, and righteous companionship, with the murabbī (teacher) serving as the binder and guide of all three means.

## Key Arguments

- The ten attributes of the promised generation cannot be assumed artificially; they require internal construction through deep knowledge, just as fruit requires prior stages of cultivation (p. 98)
- The principle "treatment is proportionate to knowledge" governs all dealings with Allah — the more one knows Him, the more sincere, loving, and fearful one becomes (pp. 99–100)
- Ignorance of Allah is the root cause of cold and limited dealings with Him; Allah says: {وَمَا قَدَرُوا اللَّهَ حَقَّ قَدْرِهِ} (6:91) (pp. 99–100)
- The five ma'ārif are the foundational axes from which the attributes grow: knowledge of Allah, self, dunya, ākhirah, and proper understanding of religion (pp. 109, 112)
- Knowledge must be deep, rooted in yaqīn, affect the emotions, and become an intrinsic part of īmān — superficial knowledge produces no change (pp. 102, 108)
- The heart is pulled by two forces: īmān and hawā; actions follow whichever is stronger at the moment of decision (p. 103)
- I'tidāl (moderation/balance) is the fruit of proper understanding (fiqh) of the religion — it prevents the excess that strong īmān and zuhd might otherwise drive one toward (pp. 110, 139)
- Ukhuwwah (brotherhood) is a natural fruit of īmān and requires an environment (bi'ah) and gatherings to be practised and deepened (pp. 110–111, 141–142)
- The three basic means for acquiring the five ma'ārif are: the Qur'an, reflecting on life events, and righteous companionship; the murabbī binds and guides all three (pp. 142–144)

## Sahabah Examples

- **Mūsā (عليه السلام)**: His request to see Allah at the mountain (7:143); when the mountain crumbled, his knowledge increased and he repented — demonstrating that increased ma'rifah produces better treatment of Allah (pp. 101–102)
- **The Anṣār**: When īmān took hold of their hearts, they loved the Muhājirūn and preferred them over themselves despite their own poverty (59:9) — demonstrating ukhuwwah as fruit of īmān (pp. 110–111)
- **'Umar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb**: His rivalry with Abū Bakr in spending for Allah's sake — righteous companionship spurs competition in good (p. 133)
- **Abū Bakr al-Ṣiddīq**: Gave all his wealth; when asked what he left for his family, he said "Allah and His Messenger" — the model of complete īmān (p. 133)
