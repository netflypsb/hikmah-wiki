## Summary

Chapter 1 answers the fundamental question: why does the ummah need the promised generation? It opens by describing the current state of humiliation, weakness, and fragmentation of the Muslim ummah — enemies attack from all sides, kill our children, destroy our homes, and detain our youth, all under the world's watchful eyes (p. 10). The material and technological superiority of the enemies makes追赶 difficult, and they will not allow the Muslims to approach parity, let alone surpass them (p. 11). However, the calculations of the people of īmān differ: there exists a power beyond human comprehension — the divine power (قوة الجبار) that owns the winds, storms, thunder, lightning, earthquakes, and even the breath of our enemies. This was the power that sustained the weak among Banī Isrā'īl against Pharaoh (7:137). Hope in Allah alone does not mean abandoning material means; rather, Muslims must fill every available space, strive in every sector, and acquire all means of power — not because these means will bring victory, but because Allah requires it (8:60). Victory comes only from Allah (8:10, 3:160). The chapter then delineates the conditions for victory (13:11, 47:7, 3:120), the need for deep internal change, the role of the promised generation in breathing life into the ummah, directing its sentiments toward true change (p. 14–15), leading it through periods of hardship and siege (p. 16–17), and ultimately through periods of prosperity and global leadership (p. 20). It draws extensively on the model of the first generation — the Ṣaḥābah in Mecca and Medina — who endured persecution, siege, and battle before receiving Allah's promise (24:55). The chapter closes by emphasising that even after the promised generation achieves dominance, it must continue building future generations and guard against the diseases of luxury, injustice, and distance from Allah (p. 21–22).

## Key Arguments

- The ummah suffers unprecedented humiliation — enemies attack from all sides, while Muslims are fragmented and powerless (p. 10).
- Material and technological calculations confirm the difficulty of the situation, but the calculations of īmān reveal an incomparable divine power (p. 11).
- Hope in Allah does not mean abandoning material means; rather, Muslims must acquire all means of power as a condition Allah requires, while knowing that victory comes only from Him (8:60, 8:10, 3:160) (p. 12).
- Victory has conditions: Allah does not change a people's condition until they change what is in themselves (13:11); if you help Allah, He will help you (47:7); patience and taqwā neutralise enemy plots (3:120) (p. 13).
- A deep, fundamental change is needed — not superficial — that returns the ummah to Allah (p. 13).
- A generation must emerge to breathe life and hope into the ummah and guide its sentiments toward true change (p. 14–15).
- When signs of awakening appear, enemies will unite in a comprehensive boycott and siege against the ummah (p. 16).
- The promised generation must lead the ummah during the period of hardship, providing for people's needs, sustaining their morale, and binding them to Allah (p. 17).
- These stages mirror what the first generation — the Ṣaḥābah — experienced: persecution, siege, threats, and war (p. 17–18).
- The period of hardship serves as a test of the ummah's sincerity and a final crucible before the birth of a strong generation (29:2–3, 2:214) (p. 19).
- After hardship comes relief; the promised generation must then work to unite the ummah, establish the khilāfah, and recover lost lands (p. 20).
- The generation must continue spreading Islam and strive for global leadership, as the first generation did (p. 20).
- Even after achieving dominance, the promised generation must not put down its weapons or grow complacent — it must continue building generations and guarding against luxury, oppression, and distance from Allah (p. 21–22).
- The ummah's need for the promised generation is urgent and demands the pooling of all efforts (p. 22).

## Sahabah Examples

- **Abū al-ʿĀliyyah's tafsīr of 24:55:** The Prophet ﷺ and his companions were in Mecca for about ten years, calling to Allah secretly, living in fear, not commanded to fight. After the Hijrah they were commanded to fight and went to sleep and woke with weapons — until a companion asked if they would always live in fear. The Prophet ﷺ gave glad tidings, and 24:55 was revealed; Allah established His Prophet over the Arabian Peninsula and they became secure (p. 18).
- **Al-Barā' ibn ʿĀzib:** Testified that 24:55 was revealed while the believers were in severe fear (p. 18).
- **ʿAbd al-Raḥmān ibn ʿAwf (رضي الله عنه):** Brought food while fasting, then wept upon remembering martyred companions — Muṣʿab ibn ʿUmayr (shrouded in a single garment) and Ḥamzah — and feared that their good deeds had been hastened as reward while later generations received the world's expansion (Bukhārī) (p. 20–21).
- **Khabbāb ibn al-Aratt (رضي الله عنه):** When visiting the ailing ʿAbdullāh ibn Masʿūd, the companions told him to rejoice — his brothers would be raised on the Day of Judgment as martyrs. He wept and said he was not anxious about death, but feared that the reward of their deeds might be what was given to those who came after them (Ḥayāt al-Ṣaḥābah 1/100–101) (p. 21).
