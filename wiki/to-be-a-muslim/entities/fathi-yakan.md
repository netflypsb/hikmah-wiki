# Fathi Yakan

## Overview

Fathi Yakan is the author of *"To Be a Muslim,"* the primary source text of this wiki. He is a Lebanese Islamic scholar and activist, founder of the Islamic Front in Lebanon, and a leading figure in the [[islamic-movement]] tradition. The book synthesizes theology, personal piety, and political activism into a unified framework for what it means to be a Muslim — in belief, worship, character, and organized struggle.

## Role in the Book

Yakan structures *"To Be a Muslim"* in two parts:

1. **Part I — The Muslim Individual**: Covers the essential dimensions of being a Muslim — [[aqidah]] (creed), [[ibadah]] (worship), morals, family life, and self-control. Yakan argues that many people are Muslim by identity and birth but lack genuine understanding of Islam's requirements, leading secular lives disconnected from its principles.

2. **Part II — The Muslim Activist**: Addresses the collective responsibility to work for Islam through organized activism. Yakan explains the nature, goals, strategy, and commitment requirements of the [[islamic-movement]], including [[baiah]] (pledge of allegiance) and [[brotherhood]].

Yakan frames the failure of contemporary Muslim societies as a consequence of importing alien man-made systems — both capitalist and socialist — that lack a solid foundation. He compares these to a rejected kidney transplant: intolerable in the long run because they do not represent the Muslim community's authentic identity.

## Key Ideas and Contributions

### The Unity of Belief and Action

Yakan insists that being a Muslim is not merely a matter of identity or private worship. Correct [[aqidah]] must produce correct action — personal piety and collective activism are inseparable. A Muslim who holds faith but does not act on it is, in Yakan's framework, a "functional atheist": someone who claims belief but lives by materialist values.

### Critique of Superficial Movements

In section 2.4, Yakan distinguishes the [[islamic-movement]] from four inadequate types of organizations:
- **Spiritual groups** that neglect politics, strategy, and external engagement
- **Ecumenical groups** that compromise Islamic principles for interfaith dialogue
- **Welfare organizations** limited to charity without systemic transformation
- **Political movements** that operate under Islam's name but violate its principles

### The Nature of Commitment

Yakan details commitment (section 2.5) as requiring:
- Absorption of [[aqidah]] as ideology, not superficial membership
- Binding one's future to the movement's future — no conditional or time-limited commitment
- Intentionality: commitment must be for Allah's sake, not from enthusiasm, pressure, or self-interest

### Bai'ah and Organizational Structure

Drawing on [[hasan-al-banna]]'s framework, Yakan presents [[baiah]] as an oath of loyalty rooted in Prophetic tradition (the pledges of al-Aqabah and ar-Ridwan). He outlines ten principles of bai'ah: understanding, sincerity, [[ibadah]] (worship through action), jihad, sacrifice, obedience, perseverance, integrity, brotherhood, and trust.

### Strategic Principles

Yakan advocates a gradualist strategy (also drawn from [[hasan-al-banna]]'s three stages — introductory, preparatory, execution), moral consistency (the end never justifies the means), and "work in public but organize in secrecy."

## Intellectual Influences

Yakan draws heavily on two major 20th-century Islamic thinkers:

- **[[hasan-al-banna]]** — Founder of the Muslim Brotherhood. Yakan cites al-Banna extensively on: the classification of materialist attitudes toward life, the five tools of the Islamic movement (love of Allah, Qur'an as universal guide, brotherhood, reliance on Allah, confidence in divine selection), the three stages of da'wah, the ten principles of bai'ah (understanding, sincerity, ibadah, jihad, sacrifice, obedience, perseverance, integrity, brotherhood, trust), the detailed responsibilities of movement members, and the role of material strength.

- **[[syed-qutb]]** — Egyptian Islamic thinker and author of *Milestones*. Yakan adopts Qutb's concept of **psychological isolation** (section 2.3.3): the idea that Muslims must be "in this environment but isolated from it" — not physically withdrawn, but mentally and morally independent of jahili society. Yakan clarifies that this isolation is distinct from nihilist or rejectionist withdrawal; it means maintaining moral independence while remaining engaged with society.
